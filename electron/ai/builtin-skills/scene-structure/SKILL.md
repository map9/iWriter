# Scene Structure

Use this skill when planning or drafting a fiction scene.

## A useful scene has

- A viewpoint character with a concrete short-term goal.
- Opposition that pressures the goal.
- A turn: the situation changes, a choice is forced, or new information lands.
- A result that leaves the story in a different state than it entered.

## Quality standard

A scene that only advances plot is doing the minimum. A strong scene simultaneously advances plot, reveals something about a character that was not visible before this pressure, and moves the emotional state of the reader. If removing the scene only removes an event and leaves the reader's understanding of the characters unchanged, the scene is underperforming.

## The turn

The turn is not simply a change of events. The best turns force a character to make a choice that reveals who they really are, or land information that reframes what the reader thought they knew. After the turn, the character—and the reader—cannot return to the position they held at the scene's opening.

## Anti-patterns

- A scene where the character's goal is met without meaningful resistance.
- A scene where the only outcome is information transfer—what could have been dialogue becomes a scene, but the character's inner state is unchanged.
- A turn that arrives through external coincidence rather than character choice or consequence.
- A scene that ends in the same emotional register it began.

## Before drafting, check

- What does the character want right now, specifically?
- What makes getting it difficult—and does that difficulty emerge from the situation, from another character's opposing need, or from the character's own limitations?
- What emotional or relational state changes by the end?
- What new fact, shifted relationship, or open question should remain after the scene closes?
