# Deep POV

Use this skill when writing from a close viewpoint.

## Principles

- Filter all description through what the viewpoint character would notice, in the order they would notice it, in the language they would use.
- Avoid reporting facts the viewpoint character cannot know.
- In high-emotion moments, prefer embodied perception over detached explanation.
- Let word choice reflect the character's mood, assumptions, and education level—not the narrator's.
- Use summary for transitions; stay close during choices, conflict, and revelation.

## What "filtering" means in practice

Filtering is not just removing impossible knowledge—it is ensuring that what the character perceives reveals who they are. Two characters looking at the same room see different things. The reader should be able to identify the viewpoint character from the specific objects noticed, the language used to describe them, and the order in which they appear.

Weak filter: "The room was cluttered and smelled of old paper. She felt uneasy."
Strong filter: "Stacked paper everywhere, and she was already calculating which pile had been touched most recently."
—Same facts, but the second tells you something about how this person processes environments.

## Emotion through perception, not label

At moments of strong emotion, the character does not know they are afraid or angry—they know their hands are cold, the other person's voice has gone flat, they have rehearsed this moment and none of the rehearsed lines are available. Render the physical and perceptual experience; let the reader name the emotion.

## Anti-patterns

- "She felt" followed by an emotion word in a high-stakes scene.
- The character accurately perceiving another character's internal state as fact, not inference.
- Narration that sounds like a neutral observer rather than a person in a particular mood.
- Description that is visually accurate but emotionally inert.

## Checklist

- Who is the viewpoint character, and what do they know, not know, and believe wrongly?
- What sensory details matter to them in this moment—not to a camera, to this person?
- Which emotions should be rendered through perception and action rather than named?
- Does the word choice sound like this character, or like a narrator describing them?
